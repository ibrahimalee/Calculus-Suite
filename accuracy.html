How often is it right?

Of 918 problems, 903 were answered correctly, 15 were declined with an explanation, and none were answered wrongly. Every number on this page is produced by a script anyone can run, against answers the engine did not supply.

03 Photos

Reading working from a photo

The “Scan a photo” button reads printed or typed maths on your device and puts the text in the box for you to check. We measured how well it does on eight short worked solutions: 92.8% of characters correct in clean print, 86.8% in a slanted serif font and 72.4% in a handwriting-like style, with 3, 2 and 0 of 8 solutions read perfectly. Even clean print can lose an exponent or a minus sign, and a misread character would look like a mistake in your working. So the app does not claim to read handwriting, it always shows you the text to correct before it is used, and it says so in the scan window. The test is scripts/ocr-benchmark.js in the repository.

04 Method

How it is tested

The problems are generated from a fixed random seed (20261123), so every run uses the same ones. The right answers never come from the engine being tested: the test script has its own differentiator, its own numerical integrator, known textbook limits, roots built from chosen integers, and matrix products to check inverses.

  • Derivatives. Random compositions of sin, cos, exp, ln, √, arctan, powers, sums, products and quotients. Each answer is compared with an independent numerical derivative at 7 points.
  • Indefinite integrals. Integrands that are known to have elementary antiderivatives (they are derivatives of random functions). Each answer is differentiated numerically and compared with the integrand.
  • Definite integrals. Random functions on random finite intervals. Each value is compared with an independent 1,280-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature.
  • Limits. Classic limits with values known from textbooks, including one-sided limits and limits at infinity.
  • Equations. Polynomials built from chosen integer roots (the answer must be exactly those roots), and equations with mixed trigonometric and polynomial terms (the answer must match an independent sign-change scan on [−10, 10]).
  • Matrices. Random integer matrices. Determinants are compared with an independent computation; inverses must multiply back to the identity; singular matrices must be reported as singular.
  • Taylor series. Random functions expanded about 0 to order 5. The polynomial must agree with the function at x = 0.02, where the first dropped term is far below the tolerance.

What this does not show

It does not prove the tool is right on every input. The problems are random compositions of standard functions and classic textbook cases, not a sample of everything students meet, and a numerical check can in principle miss an answer that is wrong only at a point or two. Treat it as strong evidence, not a guarantee, and report anything you find: open an issue with the input.
