How it works

The tool tries the standard techniques in turn: a table of known integrals, u-substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions for rational functions, and a computer-algebra fallback. The steps name the technique and show the choice of u, or of u and dv.

Some functions, such as e^(x²), have no elementary antiderivative. The tool says so instead of returning something wrong, and can still give definite integrals numerically.

How the answer is checked

Every antiderivative is differentiated and compared with your function at many points before it is shown.

Typing your input

  • Leave both limits empty for an indefinite integral. Limits accept pi, e and inf.
  • Leave both limits empty for an indefinite integral; the answer includes + C.
  • Use inf and -inf for infinite limits.
  • Use the Variable option for integrals in t, u or another letter.

Questions

Does it show which method was used?

Yes: substitution, by parts, partial fractions or a table entry, with the working on the Steps tab.

Why does it say there is no elementary antiderivative?

Some integrals, like ∫e^(x²) dx or ∫sin(x)/x dx, cannot be written with the usual functions. That is a mathematical fact, not a limitation of the tool; definite versions can still be computed numerically.
