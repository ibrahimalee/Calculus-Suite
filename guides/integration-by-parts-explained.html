Integration by parts, explained

Integration by parts undoes the product rule. Use it when the integrand is a product of two different kinds of function, like x·eˣ or x·sin x.

The formula

Split the integrand into u and dv. Then differentiate u and integrate dv.

∫udv=uv−∫vdu

Choosing u: LIATE

Pick u from the earliest of: Logarithmic, Inverse trigonometric, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential. Whatever is left is dv. The aim is for ∫v du to be simpler than the original integral.

Example

For ∫ x·eˣ dx take u = x (algebraic) and dv = eˣ dx. Then du = dx and v = eˣ, which leaves a simple integral.

Example. ∫ x·eˣ dx

When the logarithm is the whole integrand

The integral of ln x looks like it has no product, but write it as 1·ln x and take u = ln x.

Example. ∫ ln x dx

ln(x)
∫ln(x)dx=x⋅(ln(x)−1)+C

Repeating the method

For x²·eˣ you apply the method twice, because the first round leaves x·eˣ.

x2⋅ex
∫x2⋅exdx=2⋅exp(x)−2⋅x⋅exp(x)+x2⋅exp(x)+C

Questions

What if the integral comes back to itself?

With eˣ·sin x, two rounds of parts give the original integral again; you solve for it algebraically. The calculator handles that case.
