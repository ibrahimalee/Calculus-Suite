Riemann sums and the definite integral

A definite integral is the area under a curve. A Riemann sum approximates it with rectangles, and the integral is what the sums approach as the rectangles get thinner.

Adding rectangles

Split the interval [a, b] into n pieces of width Δx. On each piece draw a rectangle whose height is the function value at the left end, the right end or the midpoint. The total area of the rectangles is the sum.

Which rule is best?

Midpoint and trapezoid sums are usually far closer than left or right sums for the same n, and Simpson’s rule is closer still for smooth functions.

The fundamental theorem

If F is an antiderivative of f, then the integral from a to b is F(b) − F(a). This turns an infinite sum into a subtraction.

When there is no antiderivative

For functions like e^(−x²), numerical rules are the only way to get a value. Increase n until the digits stop changing.
