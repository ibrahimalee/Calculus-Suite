The chain rule, explained

The chain rule is for functions inside functions, such as sin(x²) or e^(3x+1). It is the rule people most often get wrong, because the inside derivative is easy to forget.

The idea

If y depends on u and u depends on x, then a small change in x changes u, which changes y. The rates multiply.

dxdy​=dudy​⋅dxdu​

A method that works every time

Name the inside function u. Differentiate the outside with respect to u, then multiply by du/dx. Finally replace u by the original expression.

Example. Differentiate e^(3x + 1): outside eᵘ, inside u = 3x + 1

e3 x+1
f′(x)=3⋅e3 x+1

✓ Checked: agrees with an independent numerical differentiation at 5 sample points.

Nested layers

With three layers, such as sin(x²) squared, apply the rule twice, working from the outside in.

Example. Differentiate sin²(x²)

sin(x2)2
f′(x)=4⋅x⋅cos(x2)⋅sin(x2)

The usual mistake

Forgetting to multiply by the derivative of the inside. The derivative of cos(2x) is −2·sin(2x), not −sin(2x).

Example. Differentiate cos(2x)

cos(2 x)
f′(x)=−(2⋅sin(2 x))

✓ Checked: agrees with an independent numerical differentiation at 5 sample points.

Questions

How do I know when to use the chain rule?

Whenever the argument of a function is anything other than a plain x: 2x, x², x + 1, eˣ and so on.
