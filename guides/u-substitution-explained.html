u-substitution, explained

Substitution undoes the chain rule. If you can see a function and, somewhere else in the integrand, its derivative, substitution turns a hard integral into an easy one.

Spot the pattern

In ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx the inside function x² has derivative 2x, and 2x is sitting right there. That is the signal.

Example. ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx, with u = x²

2 x⋅cos(x2)
∫2 x⋅cos(x2)dx=sin(x2)+C

The steps

Let u be the inside function. Compute du = u′ dx. Rewrite the whole integral in terms of u, integrate, then replace u by its expression in x.

With a constant factor

If the derivative is only off by a constant, adjust for it. For ∫ x·e^(x²) dx, du = 2x dx, so x dx = du/2.

Definite integrals

Either change the limits to u-values and never go back to x, or convert back before using the original limits. Mixing the two is the usual error.

Questions

What if there is no derivative of the inside present?

Try another technique: parts, partial fractions or a trigonometric identity.
